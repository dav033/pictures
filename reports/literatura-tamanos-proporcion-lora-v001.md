# Literature review: relative size/proportion captioning for the Sempertex balloon LoRA

Date: 2026-08-26
Scope: stress-test the architecture decision "teach relative balloon size/proportion via free-text comparative captions" against real published literature (papers, technical blog posts, benchmark results), in response to concerns raised by an internal LLM council (see `reports/council-tamanos-proporcion-lora-v001.md`).

Method note: this review was built with live web search and page fetches (arXiv abstracts/HTML, ACL Anthology, GitHub repos, HuggingFace/fal.ai/Civitai blog posts) done in this session. Every claim below is tagged with a confidence label:

- **Well-established** — corroborated by 2+ independent sources (papers, benchmarks, or a paper + independent practitioner reports pointing the same direction).
- **Single source** — found in exactly one paper/post; directionally credible but not independently corroborated. Take with caution.
- **No direct evidence found** — searched specifically for this and did not find a source; stated explicitly rather than invented.

Where a fetch tool truncated or summarized a paper (some PDFs exceeded the fetch size limit and had to be read via HTML mirrors or search-engine summaries rather than the full text), that is noted.

## Q1 — Do diffusion models have a documented weakness for counting/numeracy/relative size, and is it architectural (text encoder) rather than fixable with more data?

**Finding: Yes, this is a well-established, heavily benchmarked weakness, and there is now a dedicated benchmark category for exactly "relative size" prompts.**

- **GenEval** (Ghosh et al., "GenEval: An Object-Focused Framework for Evaluating Text-to-Image Alignment," NeurIPS 2023, [arXiv:2310.11513](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.11513)) reports per-task accuracy for SD v2.1, SDXL, and DeepFloyd IF-XL. Single-object and color tasks score 0.81–0.98, but **position** scores only **0.07–0.15** and **counting** scores **0.39–0.66** for these models. A later community re-run reports Flux-Dev at position 0.185 / counting 0.716, and Flux-Schnell at position 0.263 / counting 0.597 — position remains the weakest axis even for FLUX-family models. **Confidence: well-established** (primary benchmark paper + independently reproduced numbers for FLUX).

- **HRS-Bench** (Bakr, Sun, Shen, Khan, Li, Elhoseiny, ICCV 2023, [arXiv:2304.05390](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.05390)) is the one benchmark found that has a **dedicated "size" category** (501 prompts), directly on point for this project. Prompts are phrased like *"a banana which is smaller than a person and bigger than a car"*, scored by comparing detected bounding-box dimensions against the specified relation, at three difficulty tiers (2, 3, 4 objects). Result: the best model (DALL-E 2) reaches only **29.94% accuracy at the easy tier (2 objects)**, and **every evaluated model — including DALL-E 2, SD v1, SD v2, GLIDE, CogView2, Paella, minDALL-E, DALL-E-mini — drops to 0% at medium and hard tiers (3–4 objects)**. This is arguably the single most directly relevant data point in this entire review: it is the closest published analogue to "put a large balloon and small balloons in the same frame and expect the right relative size," and even 2-object comparisons succeed well under a third of the time on 2023-era models. **Confidence: well-established** for the general result (multiple benchmarks agree diffusion models fail spatial/size composition); this specific 501-prompt "size" category and its numbers come from one paper, so treat the exact percentages as **single source**, but the qualitative conclusion (size comparison is a severe, near-complete failure mode, worse than color/single-object) is corroborated by GenEval, T2I-CompBench, and DrawBench below.

- **T2I-CompBench / T2I-CompBench++** (Huang, Duan, Sun, Xie, Li, Liu; NeurIPS 2023 + TPAMI extension, [arXiv:2307.06350](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.06350), [GitHub](https://github.com/Karine-Huang/T2I-CompBench)) is the standard compositional benchmark. Its actual category list (confirmed from the repo README, not press summaries) is: color, shape, texture, 2D/3D spatial, non-spatial relations, numeracy, and complex compositions — **it does not have a dedicated "attribute comparison / scale / height / weight" category**, contrary to a garbled auto-summary this search process initially surfaced. Reported spatial scores are in the 0.12–0.22 range, in line with GenEval's position scores. **Correction/clarification noted explicitly**: I want to flag that an early search-engine summary claimed T2I-CompBench has a "scale/height/weight attribute comparison" category; on fetching the actual repo README this was not confirmed and appears to be a search-summarizer hallucination. I am excluding that claim and relying on HRS-Bench's "size" category instead, which is real and verified from the paper's own text via an HTML mirror.

- **DrawBench** (Saharia et al., "Photorealistic Text-to-Image Diffusion Models with Deep Language Understanding" / Imagen paper, Google, 2022) — 200 prompts across 11 categories including counting, spatial relations, and unusual object interactions; widely cited as one of the first curated benchmarks to isolate these failure categories. **Confidence: well-established** as a category-defining benchmark, though exact numeric scores per model were not retrievable from the pages fetched in this session (only category-level descriptions).

- **Root cause — is it architectural?** Two independent lines of evidence point toward "yes, at least partly, not purely a data-volume problem":
  1. Kamath, Hessel, Chang, **"Text encoders bottleneck compositionality in contrastive vision-language models"** (EMNLP 2023, [arXiv:2305.14897](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14897) / [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.301/)). They show CLIP-style text encoders compress a caption into a single vector, and probe how much compositional information (object relations, attribute binding, counting, negation) survives that bottleneck; a "text-only retrievability" score computed from that bottleneck predicts overall model compositional performance. This is a structural/architectural argument, not a data-volume one — the single-vector contrastive objective itself discards compositional detail. **Confidence: well-established** (peer-reviewed, EMNLP 2023, plus a related documented finding that CLIP has a known "quantity bias" and cannot reliably count objects such as stars in images — see the "Can CLIP Count Stars?" line of work cited by later papers).
  2. Guo et al., **"Text-to-Image Diffusion Models Cannot Count, and Prompt Refinement Cannot Help"** (2025, [arXiv:2503.06884](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.06884)) introduce T2ICountBench and explicitly test whether better/refined prompts fix counting — they find **prompt refinement does not meaningfully improve counting accuracy**, and frame this as an "inherent challenge in numerical understanding within diffusion models" rather than a prompt-engineering problem. **Confidence: single source** for the "prompt refinement doesn't help" claim specifically, but it is consistent with (1) above and with HRS-Bench's near-total failure at 3-4 objects regardless of phrasing.
  3. A partial counterpoint: switching text encoders does help *some*. eDiff-I (NVIDIA, [arXiv:2211.01324](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.01324)) and the Imagen paper both report that T5-XXL embeddings produce better prompt fidelity than CLIP alone. But this is not a full fix: **CoMPaSS** (Zhang et al., ICCV 2025, [arXiv:2412.13195](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.13195)) specifically tested whether large text encoders understand spatial relations by checking if logically-equivalent paraphrases ("A left of B" vs. its rewrite) get embedded similarly, and found **even T5-XXL (11B params) fails this test** — the problem is not just encoder size but how contrastive/frozen text encoders represent relational language at all. CoMPaSS's own fix (data curation + token-order-preserving encoding) improved GenEval Position by +131% and T2I-CompBench Spatial by +67%, which itself is evidence of *how bad the baseline is* (a improvement of that magnitude off a very low base). **Confidence: single source** for the specific T5-XXL paraphrase-failure test, but directionally consistent with the broader literature.
  4. **Relevant nuance for this project specifically**: the target endpoint is `fal-ai/flux-2-trainer-v2`, i.e. FLUX.2. FLUX.1 used a CLIP+T5-XXL dual encoder; **FLUX.2 switched to a single encoder, Mistral Small 3.1** (confirmed via HuggingFace's FLUX-2 diffusers blog post and Black Forest Labs' own architecture description). This is a genuinely different architecture from the CLIP-bottlenecked models that most of the above benchmarks were run on — Mistral Small 3.1 is a decoder-only LLM, not a frozen contrastively-trained encoder like CLIP or T5. **No direct evidence was found in this search session benchmarking FLUX.2 specifically on GenEval-position, HRS-Bench-size, or T2I-CompBench-spatial** — the FLUX numbers found above (position 0.185–0.263) are for FLUX.1 (Dev/Schnell), not FLUX.2. This is an honest gap: whether Mistral-based conditioning meaningfully closes the size/spatial gap versus CLIP/T5 is plausible by analogy (LLM encoders generally parse relational language better than CLIP) but **not directly verified for FLUX.2 in this review**.

**Bottom line for Q1**: the weakness is real, well-documented across at least three independent benchmarks (GenEval, HRS-Bench, T2I-CompBench/DrawBench lineage), and has a credible architectural component (text-encoder bottleneck), not just a "not enough data" problem. It is not fully architecture-proof, though — better encoders and targeted fine-tuning data measurably help (see Q3), they just don't solve it outright.

## Q2 — For LoRA/DreamBooth fine-tuning, how much data/repetition is needed to learn a comparative textual pattern as a generalizable rule vs. memorizing a handful of pairs? Is 60–140 examples plausible?

**This is the question with the thinnest direct evidence. Nothing in the literature answers it precisely; the honest answer is that this is a genuinely open, unmeasured question, and the closest analogues are mixed.**

- Original **DreamBooth** (Ruiz et al., 2022, arXiv:2208.12242) and **Textual Inversion** papers demonstrate subject-identity learning from as few as **3–5 images**. This is frequently cited as "LoRA needs very little data," but it is a different problem: learning to reproduce *one fixed subject's appearance*, not learning a *generalizable comparative relation* that must transfer across many different balloon colors/shapes/scenes. **Confidence: well-established** for the 3-5 image number itself, but **not directly applicable** to the comparative-pattern-learning question — this is an important distinction the literature itself does not usually make explicit, so treat the common practitioner shorthand "LoRA only needs a few images" as answering a different question than the one this project needs answered.

- Practitioner/industry guidance on LoRA dataset size varies widely and is not about relational-pattern learning either: fal.ai's own FLUX style-LoRA guide (["Training FLUX Style LoRA on fal"](https://blog.fal.ai/training-flux-style-lora-on-fal-ai)) reports acceptable results from as few as 9 curated images for a *style*; other practitioner guides recommend 10–50 for a *subject/product*; a HuggingFace diffusers advanced-training README notes that in one informal test, smaller sets (20-30 images) gave *more* prompting flexibility than larger sets, with 15 additional images notably reducing flexibility — but again, none of this is measuring "does the model learn a comparative *rule*." **Confidence: single source per claim; these are practitioner reports, not controlled studies**, and none isolate the comparative-language question.

- The closest controlled academic evidence on **data volume vs. rule-learning (rather than memorization) for a compositional pattern** is Jeong, Uselis, Laina, Oh, Rohrbach, **"When Do Diffusion Models Learn to Generate Multiple Objects?"** (2026, [arXiv:2605.00273](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.00273)), which uses a controlled synthetic framework (MOSAIC) to separately test (a) learning individual concepts under data imbalance and (b) learning *combinations* of concepts. Their key finding: **"scene complexity plays a dominant role rather than concept imbalance, and counting is uniquely difficult to learn in low-data regimes,"** and compositional generalization to *held-out* concept combinations **collapses** as more combinations are withheld — simply scaling data within the same distribution is described elsewhere in the same research thread as "insufficient" for compositional generalization (a related 2025 paper, "Does Data Scaling Lead to Visual Compositional Generalization?", [arXiv:2507.07102](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.07102), reports **60–80 percentage-point accuracy drops on unseen combinations despite near-perfect in-distribution performance**). This is a warning sign specifically relevant to this project: even large-scale controlled training struggles to generalize a compositional rule to combinations not literally seen, and this project's 60–140 examples with real-world variability (different balloon colors, shapes, product codes, scenes) is a much harder and much smaller-scale version of exactly this problem. **Confidence: single source for the exact framework, but the general "compositional generalization is harder than concept memorization, and doesn't scale linearly with data" conclusion appears consistently across this cluster of 2025-2026 papers.**

- A directly relevant *positive* data point on the other side: Chatterjee et al., **SPRIGHT / "Getting it Right: Improving Spatial Consistency in Text-to-Image Models"** (ECCV 2024, [arXiv:2404.01197](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.01197)) built a 6M-image spatially-recaptioned dataset, then found that **fine-tuning on only ~0.25% of it (≈15,000 images) produced a 22% improvement in spatially-accurate generation**, and separately report achieving a new SOTA T2I-CompBench spatial score with fine-tuning on **fewer than 500 images**. This is the single most encouraging data point found for "can a relatively modest fine-tuning set fix a spatial/relational weakness" — but 500 images of *dedicated, curated, spatial-relation-only* data is still roughly **4–8x more than the 60–140 relative-size-relevant examples** in this project's dataset, and SPRIGHT targets left/right/above/below position, not size/scale specifically. Similarly, **SR4G** (Salaberria et al., [arXiv:2403.00587](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.00587)) fine-tuned Stable Diffusion on a purpose-built 9.9M-pair spatial-relations dataset and got up to **+9 points on the VISOR metric**, generalizing even to unseen object pairs — again encouraging in direction, but at a dataset scale several orders of magnitude larger than 60–140. **Confidence: well-established direction ("dedicated relational fine-tuning data does help, and doesn't require full-scale pretraining volumes to move the needle") but single-source and much-larger-scale for the exact numbers**, so extrapolating down to ~100 examples is not something the literature directly validates.

**Bottom line for Q2**: There is no published study that tells us whether 60–140 examples is "enough" for LoRA to learn a generalizable comparative-size rule versus memorizing a handful of image-caption pairs — **this specific question could not be answered from literature; state this as a real gap, not a soft "maybe."** The nearest analogues cut both ways: subject-identity LoRA succeeds with very few images (encouraging, but different problem), while compositional/relational generalization research (MOSAIC, data-scaling papers) shows relational rules are specifically harder to generalize and don't follow the same easy data-efficiency curve as single-concept learning, and the most directly comparable *successful* interventions (SPRIGHT, SR4G) used 500 to ~10M curated examples, not ~100. If forced to characterize where 60-140 falls, the honest read of the adjacent literature is "plausible enough to move the needle in the right direction, but on the low end of what has actually been shown to work for relational/spatial fine-tuning, and unproven as sufficient to reach reliable generalization rather than partial memorization."

## Q3 — Is prose comparative language a good way to encode size relationships, or do spatial-conditioning methods (ControlNet, GLIGEN, bounding boxes) perform meaningfully better?

**Finding: the literature is unambiguous that structured/spatial conditioning outperforms prose-only captioning for spatial and size control — this is one of the best-supported conclusions in this whole review, though a size-specific ControlNet is emerging technology, not mainstream infrastructure yet.**

- **GLIGEN** (Li, Liu, Wu, Mu, Yang, Gao, Li, Lee — CVPR 2023, [arXiv:2301.07093](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.07093)) extends frozen pretrained diffusion models with grounding inputs (bounding boxes + entity text), and reports **zero-shot layout-to-image performance on COCO/LVIS that "substantially outperforms existing supervised layout-to-image baselines."** This is a direct existence proof that explicit box conditioning (which encodes both position *and* scale, since box size directly encodes object scale) is a stronger signal than prose alone for spatial/size fidelity. **Confidence: well-established** (widely cited, CVPR 2023, reproduced by many follow-ups).

- **ControlNet** (Zhang, Rao, Agrawala — ICCV 2023, [arXiv](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/ICCV2023/html/Zhang_Adding_Conditional_Control_to_Text-to-Image_Diffusion_Models_ICCV_2023_paper.html)) adds pixel-level spatial conditioning (edges, depth, pose, scribbles) to frozen diffusion backbones, and reports robust training even on small (<50k) datasets. ControlNet itself does not directly target "size comparison of two named objects" out of the box (its conditioning maps are usually derived from a reference image, not from text describing a scale ratio), but it is the standard proof that explicit structural conditioning is more reliable than prompt text for anything spatial. **Confidence: well-established**.

- **Directly on point**: Boudier & Caselles-Dupré, **"Proportion and Perspective Control for Flow-Based Image Generation"** (2025, [arXiv:2510.21763](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.21763)) build a **"Proportion ControlNet"** specifically for flow-based models (the FLUX family's architecture) that uses bounding boxes to dictate *both position and scale* of objects, precisely the "large balloon vs. small balloon" use case. This is, as far as this search found, the closest thing in the literature to a purpose-built solution for exactly this project's core requirement. The abstract confirms the module "provides effective control but exhibits limitations with complex constraints" — i.e., it works but isn't perfect on hard cases. **Confidence: single source** — this is one specific project's technical report (not yet a widely-cited peer-reviewed paper as of this search), released as an open weight on HuggingFace (`obvious-research`), so treat it as a promising but early-stage/unvalidated-at-scale approach, not an established best practice.

- **Benchmark-level evidence that structured beats prose for spatial fidelity**: CoMPaSS (already cited in Q1) improved GenEval Position by +131% and T2I-CompBench Spatial by +67% primarily via **data curation (SCOP) and preserving token order (TENOR) in the *text* pipeline itself** — notably, this is a text-side fix, not a switch to bounding boxes, and it still produced large gains, suggesting that if prose captioning is done carefully (avoiding ambiguous data, preserving relational token order), it can meaningfully improve over naive captioning without going all the way to explicit spatial conditioning. Similarly, Merchant et al., **"Structured Captions Improve Prompt Adherence in Text-to-Image Models"** ([arXiv:2507.05300](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.05300)) show that captions with a **consistent structural order (subject → setting → aesthetics → camera)**, fine-tuned into PixArt-Σ / SD2 on a 19M re-captioned LAION subset, produce measurably higher text-image alignment than the same information in randomly shuffled order. Neither paper isolates numeric/size attributes specifically, but both are evidence that **prose captioning is not doomed — its internal structure and consistency matters a lot**, which is directly actionable for this project's caption-writing convention (put the comparative-size clause in a consistent position/format across all captions, rather than varying its phrasing/order freely). **Confidence: well-established** for "caption structure and data curation measurably help," **single source** for the exact numeric gains attributable to structure alone (isolating structure from data volume changes is hard, and these papers combine both interventions).

- **fal.ai's actual production tooling**: the general FLUX endpoint (`fal-ai/flux-general`) does support ControlNet conditioning (canny, depth, pose, seg, subject) alongside LoRA merging today — so a spatial/box-conditioning approach is not purely theoretical for this project's stack, though it is not integrated into the `flux-2-trainer-v2` LoRA training endpoint itself, and no off-the-shelf "balloon scale" ControlNet exists; it would have to be built (following something like the Boudier & Caselles-Dupré Proportion ControlNet approach) or approximated at inference time by manually specifying reference bounding boxes. **Confidence: single source (fal.ai's own docs)** for tooling availability; this is a factual/product claim, not a research claim.

**Bottom line for Q3**: The literature strongly and consistently favors explicit spatial/box conditioning over prose alone when the goal is precise object position or scale — this is close to consensus. However, (a) a size/scale-specific ControlNet for FLUX-style models is bleeding-edge (one 2025 technical report, not an established, widely-adopted technique), and (b) there is real evidence that *well-structured* prose captioning (consistent phrasing order, curated/unambiguous relational data) meaningfully improves outcomes even without switching to box conditioning. This suggests a **hybrid recommendation is what the literature actually supports**, not a binary "prose is wrong, use ControlNet instead": keep prose captioning but make it maximally consistent and unambiguous in structure, and treat a bounding-box/proportion-conditioning add-on as a credible upgrade path if prose-only LoRA training proves insufficient — not as something to build from scratch as a first resort.

## Q4 — Is there a real "tokenizer fragmentation" issue when mixing "18 in" / "R-12" / "9-inch" formats in captions?

**Finding: the general mechanism (numbers get fragmented into multiple subword tokens, reducing the effective learnable signal for any one numeric pattern) is well-established for language-model tokenization broadly; direct, dedicated research on CLIP/T5's specific tokenizer behavior with mixed product-code/unit formats like this project's was not found, so the size-of-the-effect for this exact case is inferred, not directly measured.**

- **Well-established, general mechanism**: BPE-style tokenizers (used by GPT-family models, and also the CLIP tokenizer that underlies SD/SDXL/FLUX.1's CLIP branch) do not treat numbers as atomic units by default. A widely cited discussion (Karpathy-style analyses summarized in ["Let's Build the GPT Tokenizer"](https://www.fast.ai/posts/2025-10-16-karpathy-tokenizers.html) and corroborated by Singh & Strouse, ICLR 2025, on right-to-left tokenization improving arithmetic by 22+ points) documents that inconsistent digit-grouping (e.g., "127" as one token but "677" split into two) measurably degrades a model's ability to learn numeric patterns, and this is specifically cited as a contributor to LLM arithmetic weaknesses. Modern LLM tokenizers (GPT-4, Llama 3) have since adopted fixed digit-chunking (0-999 as single tokens, then chunked) specifically to reduce this fragmentation. **Confidence: well-established**, but this evidence base is about **general-purpose LLM tokenizers and arithmetic**, not specifically the CLIP BPE vocabulary (49,408 tokens) used in SD/SDXL/FLUX.1's text encoder, nor Mistral Small 3.1's tokenizer used in FLUX.2.

- **No direct evidence found** for the exact claim in the council's concern — that mixing "18 in," "R-12," and "9-inch" specifically fragments the CLIP or FLUX tokenizer's vocabulary and measurably reduces learnable-pattern frequency for a diffusion LoRA. This is a plausible extrapolation from the general tokenization-fragmentation literature (mixed numeric formats almost certainly do get split into more, less-consistent subword pieces than a single canonical format would), but no paper or blog post tests this specific scenario for balloon/product-code captions, or for diffusion text encoders at all. **This should be reported as a reasonable inference from adjacent evidence, not a directly verified finding.**

- **Related, partially corroborating evidence for diffusion specifically**: the "Demystifying Numerosity in Diffusion Models" paper ([arXiv:2510.11117](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.11117)) found that even with scaling, diffusion models (including FLUX and GPT-4o-class systems) show numerosity biases tied to what the noise prior has learned, and a separate finding surfaced in this search ("the frequency of counts in LAION-2B captions correlates with the counting generation accuracy of Stable Diffusion 3") suggests that **raw frequency of a specific numeric expression in training captions does causally matter for whether the model can reproduce it** — which indirectly supports the council's underlying worry (if "R-12" and "12-inch" and "R12" are treated as different, rarer token sequences than a single canonical "R-12" used everywhere, the effective frequency of the pattern is diluted). **Confidence: single source**, and it's about counting-word frequency, not tokenizer fragmentation of unit/product-code formats specifically — it supports the *spirit* of the concern (consistency and repetition of a canonical form matters) without directly proving the *mechanism* (subword fragmentation) the council named.

**Bottom line for Q4**: The concern is **not overstated as a general principle** — tokenizer-level numeric fragmentation reducing learnable-pattern frequency is real and documented for LLM tokenizers broadly, and caption-frequency-of-a-pattern is documented to matter for diffusion numeracy specifically. But the *exact* claim ("mixing '18 in' and 'R-12' fragments this project's captions enough to matter") is an inference, not a measured finding — **no direct evidence found** for this specific scenario. The safe, low-cost, evidence-consistent mitigation (regardless of whether the effect size is large or small) is straightforward and low-risk to apply: pick one canonical numeric/unit format and use it consistently across all captions, since consistency is cheap and every adjacent piece of evidence (frequency-matters-for-counting, structured-captions-improve-alignment) points the same direction.

## Q5 — Published best practices / case studies / post-mortems specifically about training LoRAs to respect relative size/scale/proportion?

**Finding: No direct case study or post-mortem specifically about relative size/proportion LoRA training was found from Replicate, fal.ai, Civitai, HuggingFace, or Stability AI. This is the weakest-evidenced question in the review — state this honestly rather than stretching adjacent material to look like a direct answer.**

- Searches across fal.ai's blog, Replicate's blog/model READMEs, Civitai articles, and HuggingFace community blog posts for LoRA captioning guidance returned extensive general captioning advice (natural language vs. tag-based captioning, trigger words, dataset size/quality tradeoffs, avoiding style-naming, repetition signaling importance — e.g. Minta Carlson's HuggingFace post ["Enhancing Image Model Dreambooth Training Through Effective Captioning"](https://huggingface.co/blog/alvdansen/enhancing-lora-training-through-effective-captions)), but **none of these specifically address teaching a model comparative size/scale relationships between two objects in the same frame.** This absence is itself informative: relative-size LoRA training does not appear to be a well-trodden, previously-solved problem with an established best-practice writeup anywhere in the mainstream LoRA-training community content indexed by web search as of this session. **Confidence: no direct evidence found** — explicitly, not "well established that it doesn't work," just genuinely undocumented in the practitioner literature searched.

- The closest thing to relevant "practitioner case study" evidence is generic guidance that is directionally consistent with, but not proof of, the relative-size approach: fal.ai and HuggingFace guides agree that (a) captions should be as specific and consistent as possible, (b) repeated terms are learned more strongly than one-off terms, (c) mixing inconsistent data types/styles in one LoRA degrades results. All three principles argue *for* being disciplined about the comparative-size caption pattern (consistent phrasing, enough repetition, don't mix it with unrelated caption noise) but none of them were tested against a size/scale use case specifically. **Confidence: single source per claim, generic applicability only.**

- The strongest indirect "case study"-equivalent evidence is the SPRIGHT and SR4G papers already cited in Q2/Q3 — these are the only found examples of someone deliberately building a *fine-tuning dataset* to fix a specific relational weakness (spatial position, not size) in a diffusion model and publishing before/after numbers. They are academic papers, not industry blog post-mortems, and neither targets size/scale specifically, but they are the closest real analogue this review could find to "did fine-tuning on curated relational captions actually fix the relational weakness," and the answer in both cases was **yes, measurably, at their respective data scales** (500 to ~10M images depending on the paper). **Confidence: well-established that the general strategy (curate relational fine-tuning data, fine-tune) works for *some* relational property at *some* scale; no evidence, positive or negative, specifically for size/scale at the 60–140-example scale this project is operating at.**

**Bottom line for Q5**: There is no known published success story, failure story, or best-practice guide specifically about training a diffusion LoRA to respect relative object size/proportion. This project would, as far as this literature search can tell, be operating without a direct precedent to check its results against — which raises the practical importance of the project's own internal evaluation (the human-reviewed visual QA the council mentioned) since the literature cannot substitute for it here.

## Final synthesis

**Verdict: risky-but-workable, with the caveat that the risk is not evenly distributed — it is concentrated exactly where this project needs the model to be most reliable (multi-size scenes), and the project currently has no literature-backed reason for confidence at its specific data scale.**

Putting the five answers together:

1. The underlying difficulty is real and well-documented (Q1): relative size/scale composition is one of the worst-performing capabilities of text-to-image diffusion models across every benchmark that measures it (GenEval position ≈0.07–0.26, HRS-Bench size 0% at 3+ objects for 2023-era models), and there is credible architectural evidence (Kamath et al.'s text-encoder bottleneck argument, CoMPaSS's finding that even 11B-parameter T5-XXL fails paraphrase-invariance for spatial relations) that this is not purely a "not enough data" problem. This means the project should **not treat 0/401-style data-hygiene audits as evidence that the *model* will get proportions right** — they only prove the captions are internally consistent, not that the trained model will honor them, which matches a specific criticism raised in the internal council review.

2. At the same time, the literature does **not** say this is hopeless or that prose captioning categorically cannot work. Two credible mechanisms give real hope: (a) newer/larger text encoders (T5-XXL, and plausibly Mistral Small 3.1 in FLUX.2, though this specific model was not benchmarked in this review) partially — not fully — reduce the gap versus CLIP-only models; (b) dedicated relational fine-tuning (SPRIGHT, SR4G, CoMPaSS) reliably moves the needle on spatial/relational benchmarks when done with curated, consistently-structured data, even without needing full-pretraining-scale volumes.

3. The specific number this project is operating at — 60–140 examples with comparative-size language — sits in a genuine evidence gap (Q2, Q5). Nothing in the literature validates or invalidates that scale for *this exact task* (learning a generalizable relational rule, not a fixed subject). The nearest analogues that succeeded (SPRIGHT: ~500-15,000 images; SR4G: 9.9M pairs) all operated at meaningfully larger scale than 60-140, even though they were also targeting a narrower, single relational property rather than a whole style+relation combination. The nearest analogues that show *how hard* compositional generalization is (MOSAIC, the 60-80pp accuracy drop on held-out combinations paper) are a genuine warning sign for a project relying on under 140 examples to generalize across many distinct balloon shapes/colors/sizes.

4. The tokenizer-fragmentation concern (Q4) is plausible but unverified for this exact scenario — worth mitigating (pick one canonical numeric format) because the fix is nearly free, not because the literature proves the effect is large here.

**Strongest evidence-based recommendations, in order of cost/effort**:

- **Cheapest, do regardless of outcome**: canonicalize caption number/unit formatting to a single consistent style (Q4), and standardize *where in the sentence* the comparative-size clause appears and *how* it's phrased across all 60-140 relevant captions (Q3's structured-caption evidence — CoMPaSS, Merchant et al. — suggests consistency of structure meaningfully helps even without changing anything else). This is low-cost and every relevant finding points the same direction.

- **Medium cost, directly literature-supported**: treat this as fundamentally a data-quality and evaluation problem, not just a captioning-technique problem. Given that HRS-Bench shows even direct box-based evaluation of "is object A actually bigger than object B in the output" is the correct test (not text-caption-vs-order-audit), invest specifically in human visual QA that checks "does the generated image's larger balloon actually look larger" — the literature gives no reason to trust automated text-only audits as a proxy for this, and every benchmark in this review that measured *actual pixel/bounding-box size* found much worse results than instinct would suggest.

- **Higher cost, but the single most evidence-aligned mitigation if prose-only training underperforms**: a lightweight scale/proportion-conditioning add-on at inference time (bounding boxes indicating each named balloon's rough region and relative scale), following the direction of Boudier & Caselles-Dupré's Proportion ControlNet for flow-based models (2025) — this is early-stage, single-source evidence, not an established industry practice, but it is the only approach found in this entire review that is purpose-built for exactly this project's core requirement, and fal.ai's existing `flux-general` endpoint already supports ControlNet-style conditioning alongside LoRA merging, so the infrastructure gap to try it is smaller than it might seem.

- **What the literature does not support**: treating the current architecture decision as either obviously safe (no benchmark or paper found suggests relative-size composition is easy or reliably learnable from ~100 prose examples) or obviously doomed (no paper found says prose-only relational captioning cannot work at all — the SPRIGHT/SR4G/CoMPaSS results show it can, just typically at larger curated-data scale than this project currently has, and the project's task combines relation-learning with style-learning simultaneously, which is harder than either alone and outside what any single cited paper measured). The most honest characterization is: **plausible enough to be worth trying as planned, but without literature-backed confidence at this scale, and with a well-documented failure mode (relative size/count reasoning) as exactly the risk to monitor most closely and validate visually rather than assume from data-hygiene checks alone.**
